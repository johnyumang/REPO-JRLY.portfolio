| Line (sentences.json) | Claim | Status and sources |

| s12 (INDIO) | "Suki: galing sa Hokkien na 'chu-khe', ang mamimiling laging bumabalik." | Well supported, two sources: (1) Wiktionary, Tagalog "suki": borrowed from Hokkien 主客 (chú-kheh, "regular customer or dealer", literally "principal guest"), citing Gloria Chan-Yap, *Hokkien Chinese Borrowings in Tagalog* (1980); (2) an Inquirer opinion column, "Suki" (opinion.inquirer.net/108785), which cites Carstairs Douglas's Amoy dictionary of 1873: tsu-kheh, "a regular customer, either buyer or seller" (search summary only; page not opened). | s18 to s19 | Sakai, about 1594; a warehouse of jars by a canal | The date: Sukezaemon's Luzon wares reached Hideyoshi in 1593 or 1594 (WP "Luzon Sukezaemon"; a Japanese biographical entry dates the presentation to 1594, search summary in the old facts.md). | s22 to s24 (KICHI) | Jars from Luzon; the lords keep tea in them; they compete for them | Certain: "Rusun" jars, dark brown tapayan from Luzon, were sought by Japanese traders in the sixteenth century and used for tea; Antonio de Morga (1609) wrote that the Japanese found tea "can best be kept and preserved in these jars" and that "some jars are valued at, and sold for, two thousand taels" (WP "Tapayan", quoting Morga). Second source: the Inquirer columns of Ambeth Ocampo on the "Ruson-tsubo" (a letter of Hideyoshi of 1594 asking for jars; search summary only). No price is spoken |
| s25 to s26 (INDIO) | "Sa amin, lalagyan lang 'yan ng bagoong. Dito, kayamanan." | A joke on a true contrast: Morga describes them as old brown jars of the natives of Luzon whose origin nobody could explain. | s27 to s29 (KICHI); s49 to s51 | The bow that must be returned; the kappa bows back and its water spills | Kappa folklore, recorded: "Kappa are obsessed with politeness, so if a person makes a deep bow, it will return the gesture"; the water spills and it is "unable to leave the bowing position until the plate is refilled"; if the dish is spilled it "becomes severely weakened" (WP "Kappa (folklore)"). Kichi's rule about people is the child's own |
| s35 | The kappa: a water being of the Japanese, the size of a child, a dish of water on its head | Folklore: "roughly humanoid in form and about the size of a child", greenish, "a turtle-like carapace", a beaked mouth, "a depression on the head, called a 'dish' (sara)" that holds water (same article). The trivia calls it "kuwentong-bayan ng Japan" |
| s44; scene 3 stage line | It throws him into the canal like a wrestler | Folklore: kappa "pull them in with their great skill at wrestling" (same article) |
| s53 to s54, s59 | He refills the dish; he gives a cucumber; it becomes his ally | Folklore: "If a person refills it, the kappa will serve that person for all eternity"; "the cucumber as their traditional favorite meal"; a kappa keeps a pledge it has given (same article). The knot is the series' own device |
| s64 to s65 | Tondo, 1588: the chiefs plot in secret against the Spaniards | Certain: the Tondo Conspiracy, 1587 to 1588, led by Agustín de Legazpi and his cousin Martín Pangan (WP "Tondo Conspiracy"; WP "Magat Salamat") |
| s66 | Their envoy, Magat Salamat, trusted a man who pretended to be an ally | Certain in outline: Magat Salamat, a chief of Tondo (a son of Lakandula: Santiago 1990, per WP "Magat Salamat"), was the envoy to Borneo; in the Calamianes he revealed the plan to Antonio Surabao, a man of Cuyo "posing as a supporter", the servant of the encomendero Pedro Sarmiento (both articles). The line puts the deceit on the informer, as the record does |
| s67 | He informed; they were hanged | Certain: Sarmiento reported it to Governor Santiago de Vera on 26 October 1588; Legazpi and Pangan were hanged; Magat Salamat was executed ("hanged", WP "Magat Salamat"); others were exiled to New Spain or fined. | s68 to s69 (INDIO) | "Sabi ng titser ko, tatlong daang taon tayong tahimik. Ito, 1588 pa lang." | The episode's sabi-sabi, answered by the date itself: 1588 is seventeen years after Manila was taken in 1571 |
| Trivia | Morga 1609; the conspiracy and Surabao; no record puts Magat Salamat in Japan; the kappa is folklore; little is certain about Luzon Sukezaemon | Rows above. Sukezaemon: "Very little is known about him from original sources" (WP "Luzon Sukezaemon"); he does not appear in the story |

**Hero lines.** No significant real figure speaks. Magat Salamat is seen through the lens, from behind, and says
nothing; no word of his survives. The diwa in his medallion has ONE invented line under the owner's exception,
"Ang lihim, may tamang pagsasabihan. Piliin mo." (s71 to s72): it echoes the record (a plan told to the wrong
man) and adds no act and no guilt. It is not offered as a quotation, and the trivia says "hindi 'yon naitala".
Luzon Sukezaemon and Toyotomi Hideyoshi are not shown. No tender beat: no wife or partner is in the scene.

## 3. Guard rails checked for this script

- Japanese people are people: one kind, hard-working boy; no accent joke, no costume joke. The kappa keeps its
  folklore traits (courtesy, the dish, the cucumber, wrestling, a pledge kept) and is beaten by nobody: Indio
  gives its water back.
- The fall is not Episode 11's: no gold, no sunken boat, no deep water. He is thrown, by the belt, into a
  chest-deep stone canal in daylight.
- The comeback is the friend's lesson and an honest act (the bow, the water given back, his only food). It is
  not "he confessed and the dead thing relit": the confession to M comes after, as the wage he owes, as the
  bible row says.
- No real person of 1588 or 1594 has words; no guilt is put on Magat Salamat beyond the record. No living
  person, firm or brand. No sentence says anything is invented. Trivia at the end. No follow card.
- Usman is only inside the jump. The rival is not named or seen. Nothing says who the woman in white serves.
